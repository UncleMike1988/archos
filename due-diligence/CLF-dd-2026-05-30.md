## 1. Is it real? — Clean pass, with an asterisk the brief already knew: real ≠ healthy.

The fraud gate is trivial and over in one breath. CLF is a ~$7.6B NYSE primary listing, ~25,000 employees, the
largest flat-rolled steel producer in North America, vertically integrated from iron-ore pellets and HBI through
primary steelmaking to stamping/tooling/tubing, audited, with physical blast furnaces, EAFs and finishing lines
across Ohio, Pennsylvania, Indiana, West Virginia and Canada (Stelco). FY2025 revenue **$18.6B**. There is no
promotion infrastructure, no paid-IR footprint, no dilution-by-toxic-converts story. Operators are industry
lifers — CEO **Lourenco Goncalves** (running Cliffs since 2014, architect of the AK Steel and AM USA roll-ups),
CFO **Keith Koci**. **Insider behavior is a quiet positive tell:** 7 insider buys / 0 sells in the trailing 90
days (neutral-to-positive sentiment per SEC Form 4 aggregation) — management adding into the trough, not
distributing into a pump.

The asterisk, which is the whole point: **"real" here means real *and currently unprofitable and over-levered*,

The fraud gate passes immediately; the financial-risk gate is the one that does real work in this file (Sections

## 3. The GOES monopoly — real, durable, and the genuine crown jewel. Also genuinely small and capacity-capped.

This is the asset the bull case is built on, and it deserves real respect before the "but."

- **Sole US producer, full stop.** Butler Works (Butler, PA), run through CLF's wholly-owned AK Steel subsidiary,
  is the **only** domestic producer of high-silicon **grain-oriented electrical steel** — the magnetic core
  laminate inside every power and distribution transformer. Not "leading." *Only.* In all of North America. Even
  after Nippon Steel's acquisition of U.S. Steel (closed late 2024/2025), USS does not make GOES — so the monopoly
  survived the largest consolidation in the sector's history.
- **Capacity ~250,000 tons/year** of electrical steel at Butler, of which GOES is the largest single output (the
  plant also makes some non-oriented electrical steel / NOES). Exact GOES tonnage and current utilization are not
  separately disclosed; given the documented shortage and pricing, it is almost certainly running hot. **Flag the
  number that matters most: 250K tons is a *hard ceiling*.** Even a monopolist can only sell what it can make.
- **Pricing power is real and demonstrated.** GOES prices have **roughly doubled since 2020**, with delivery lead
  times of 12–18 months. US domestic GOES output meets only **~one-fifth of US demand** — the other ~80% is
  imported. So the constraint is severe *and* CLF physically cannot satisfy the home market. That is simultaneously
  the source of its pricing power (scarcity) and the cap on its upside (it can't volume-scale into the shortage
  without the 2028 project, and even then only marginally).
- **Where it sits in the P&L:** GOES/electrical is a slice of the **"3% stainless and electrical"** line of
  Q1-2026's 4.1M-ton shipment mix (44% hot-rolled, 29% coated, 15% cold-rolled, 5% plate, 3% stainless+electrical,
  4% other). On a ~16.5–17.0M-ton FY guide, **electrical steel is on the order of low-single-digit-percent of
  volume** — high-dollar, high-margin per ton, but small in the mix. This is the single most important fact for
  sizing the thesis (Section 8).

The global competitive set, for context: **Baowu, Nippon Steel, POSCO, ThyssenKrupp** are the foreign GOES
majors; POSCO ~16.8% share leads the global market; China is ~45% of global output. The global GOES market is
only ~$7.5–9.25B *in total* — a useful reality check on how much revenue the entire product category can ever
represent.

## 4. Weirton transformer plant — real, on schedule, forward-integrating the monopoly. Unit economics NOT yet company-disclosed.

CLF is repurposing the idled Weirton (WV) tinplate mill's **Half Moon Warehouse** into a **three-phase
distribution-transformer** plant. Verified facts: **$150M total capital investment**, of which **$50M is a
forgivable loan from the State of West Virginia**; **600 USW-represented jobs** recalled from the indefinitely
idled tinplate operation; targeted to **come online in H1-2026** (most recent local reporting, March 2025, still
held the 2026 timeline). The strategic logic is clean and real: the plant **consumes CLF's own GOES** — Cliffs
becomes the only US transformer maker that owns its core-steel supply, a vertically integrated lock from melt to
finished transformer at the exact moment transformer lead times have blown out to 3–5 years.

**The honest gap:** the brief's "$300K/transformer × how many units/year" is **not a company-disclosed figure.**
CLF has published the capex, jobs and timeline but **not** an annual unit capacity or per-unit price. So any
transformer-revenue number is *modeled, not verified*. A sanity frame, clearly labeled as illustrative: a $150M
single-line distribution-transformer plant might support on the order of a few thousand units/year at maturity;
at a few-hundred-thousand dollars apiece that is plausibly **$100–300M of incremental revenue** once fully ramped
— **real and accretive, but a rounding error against $18.6B of total revenue**, and years from full ramp. Do not
underwrite a precise transformer number; underwrite "a real new high-margin line that is small relative to the
whole."

## 5. Butler Works DOE expansion — verified, on track for 2028 — but it's a COST/decarbonization project, not a "+25K-ton capacity" add. Correct the brief here.

Every element the brief listed checks out *except the capacity claim*:

- **$170M total project, $75M DOE grant** (Office of Clean Energy Demonstrations). ✓ Confirmed.
- **Survived federal review and remains on schedule for 2028 completion.** ✓ Confirmed (April 2026 reporting).
- **Scope:** add **four induction reheat furnaces, retire two natural-gas furnaces** — electrifying the reheat

- **~$80M in expected annual cost savings and yield improvements** from lower energy cost, lower emissions, and
  better slab quality. ✓ Confirmed.
- **"+25,000 tons additional capacity" — NOT supported by the project description.** Every primary source frames

  modest yield-driven throughput uplift, but the brief's framing of a discrete 25K-ton capacity add appears to be

  bigger bull point than "cost savings," and the evidence supports the smaller claim.

So the correct read: Butler gets *cheaper and cleaner* in 2028, not materially *bigger*. The 250K-ton ceiling
(Section 3) essentially holds.

## 6. Section 232 tariffs — real and tightening, BUT the transformer treatment is 15% (transitional, through 2027), not 50%. This is the most consequential correction in the file.

The April 2026 restructuring is real and is helping CLF's *base steel* business materially. But the brief's
"50% tariffs including transformers" conflates two different tiers, and the distinction is load-bearing for the
GOES/transformer thesis.

The **April 2, 2026 Presidential Proclamation** (effective April 6) abandoned the old "metal-content value"
method for a **classification-based** system applying Section 232 duties to the **full entered value** of the
import, by HTS category:

- **50%** — products made completely/almost completely of *non-US* steel (commodity pipe, tube, mill products).
  **This is the tier that hits foreign GOES *as a raw mill product*** — and it's a powerful wall around CLF's
  core-steel monopoly.
- **25%** — derivative products with >15% foreign metal content by weight.
- **15% — *transitional* rate on "metal-intensive industrial equipment and electrical grid components (e.g.,
  transformers, heavy machinery)" through December 31, 2027.** **This is the tier that hits imported finished
  transformers** — only 15%, and explicitly *temporary*.
- **10%** — products made abroad with ≥95% US-origin metal.
- **Exempt** — ≤15% total metal content.

Why it matters: a foreign producer importing **GOES steel** into the US eats up to **50%** — great for CLF's
mill. But a hyperscaler or utility importing a **finished transformer** from Mexico/Korea eats only **15%** (for
now). So the tariff wall is **tall around the steel and short around the finished good** — which means the
cheapest way to dodge CLF is to import the *completed transformer*, not the steel. The 15% transitional tier
*expires end-2027*, which is a genuine future catalyst (it should step up to a higher tier), but as of today the
transformer moat is thinner and time-limited, not the 50% fortress the brief implied. Goncalves himself flagged
this approvingly on the Q1 call: *"Recent actions related to derivative products have brought needed clarity… 
supporting manufacturing in the United States."* Real tailwind — just calibrate it to 15%, not 50%, on the
finished good. (Separately, CLF has long pushed antidumping/countervailing cases on imported electrical-steel
laminations and cores — a parallel, narrower line of protection.)

The broader trade backdrop is a real, large tailwind for the *commodity* business: **steel imports are at their
lowest level since the global financial crisis**, which is exactly what's letting domestic HRC prices and CLF's
ASP recover (Section 9).

## 7. DOE efficiency standards — verified, and the amorphous-steel threat was indeed defused. A durable regulatory moat layer.

The 2023 NOPR would have effectively forced a **~95% market shift to amorphous-metal cores**, which would have
*stranded GOES*. The **final rule (effective July 8, 2024; compliance April 23, 2029)** reversed that: under the
final standards **~75% of the market can comply using GOES**, the amorphous mandate was sharply cut back, and the
compliance window was extended from 3 to 5 years. DOE's own framing: the standards "result in more demand for
core materials like grain-oriented electrical steel." This is the most *durable* moat layer — it's federal
regulation that simultaneously (a) raises minimum transformer efficiency (more GOES content per unit) and (b)
declines to mandate the substitute that would have killed GOES. Verified and real. It is the layer least exposed
to the business cycle and most exposed to *politics* (a future administration could revisit), but as written it's
a multi-year lock-in favoring the exact product CLF monopolizes.

## 8. The five-layer moat, graded honestly — and the GOES/transformer earnings contribution, sized.

Mapping the moat the brief described, then grading each layer A–C on durability/strength:

1. **Sole-domestic-producer monopoly (GOES).** *Grade A on exclusivity, C on scale.* Genuinely unique; nobody
   else makes it in North America. But capped at 250K tons and ~3% of the mix — a deep moat around a small castle.
2. **DOE efficiency regulation locking in GOES (amorphous killed).** *Grade A.* Durable, federal, multi-year,
   demand-creating. The best layer.
3. **Section 232 tariffs.** *Grade B on the steel (50% wall), C+ on the finished transformer (15%, transitional
   to end-2027).* Strong on the input, leaky and time-limited on the output.
4. **DOE / state government funding** ($75M Butler grant, $50M WV Weirton loan). *Grade B.* Real cash, lowers cost
   and signals political backing — but it's a cost subsidy, not a demand or exclusivity moat.
5. **Physical capacity constraints / lead times** (12–18mo GOES, 3–5yr transformers). *Grade A — and arguably the
   real moat.* You cannot stand up a GOES line or a transformer plant quickly; the secondary-recrystallization
   metallurgy is genuinely hard. This is what protects CLF's incumbency even where tariffs leak.

Net: it's a **real, layered, government-fortified moat** — not manufactured, not a story. The fair criticism is
not that the moat is fake; it's that **the moat protects a business line that is small relative to the enterprise
it's attached to.**

**Sizing it (the crux math).** Take the entire global GOES market: ~$8–9B of revenue *worldwide*. CLF's 250K-ton
slice, even at premium domestic monopoly pricing, is plausibly **~$700M–1.0B of GOES revenue** at a healthy
margin — call it, generously, a few hundred million of EBITDA at the segment level once fully priced and utilized,
plus an eventual **$100–300M** of transformer revenue at a good margin. Stack the Butler **$80M/yr cost savings**
(2028) on top. So the *entire* GOES + transformer + cost-savings complex might contribute on the order of
**$0.4–0.7B of through-cycle EBITDA** at maturity. Against that: the **base steel business does ~$2–3B of EBITDA
at mid-cycle** (and did ~$5B at the 2021 peak / roughly nil-to-negative now). **The chokepoint is real but is
~15–25% of normalized earnings power, not the majority.** That is the number the bull case has to live with: even
if GOES is everything the thesis says, **the steel cycle still drives the stock.**

## 9. The base steel business — why it's losing money, and the genuine inflection now underway.

**Why it's been losing money:** 2024–25 was a brutal trough — soft HRC pricing, weak automotive/industrial
demand, the dilutive early integration of acquisitions (AM USA, then Stelco), idle-facility costs, and in
Q1-2026 specifically an **$80M one-time energy-cost spike** from extreme cold weather plus price-realization lags
(contract prices reset on a lag to spot). The result was the four-year operating-income slide and FY2025's
−$1.58B operating loss.

**The inflection is real and visible in Q1-2026, and this is the live catalyst:**
- **Adjusted EBITDA +$95M** in Q1-2026 (and +$175M ex the one-time energy hit) — versus **−$179M** in Q1-2025 and
  **−$21M** in Q4-2025. That's a swing from deeply negative to solidly positive.
- **ASP $1,048/ton**, up from $980 (Q1-25) and $993 (Q4-25); **cash margin $136M** vs $25M in Q4-25.
- **Steel shipments 4.1M tons, +338k q/q**; FY guide reaffirmed at **16.5–17.0M tons**.
- **Management guided Q2-2026 to positive free cash flow** and "each quarter to improve sequentially" — a return
  to the cash-generative profile.
- The macro driver is durable, not just restocking: **imports at a post-GFC low** (Section 232), domestic supply
  discipline, and the GM "Supplier of the Year" relationship anchoring auto volume. Goldman recently nudged its
  target $9 → $10 on "stronger steel pricing backdrop and disciplined domestic supply."

So the *commodity* engine is turning up off the bottom. That's the near-term reason the stock moved from a May low
near **$10** to ~**$13.3** (+~27%) — a cycle-turn trade, with GM/Palantir headlines as accelerant. The GOES story
is the *call option on top*, not the thing moving the tape right now.

## 10. Balance sheet — the real risk, and where the brief's "~3x net debt/EBITDA" needs correcting.

This is where the hard DD bites hardest.

- **Long-term debt $7.76B; cash $45M → net debt ~$7.7B.** Debt *rose* $510M in Q1 (net ABL draw) to fund the
  loss-making quarter. Cash is razor-thin; the company runs on its **ABL revolver** for working capital.
- **Liquidity $3.1B** as of 3/31/2026 — but understand what that is: **mostly undrawn ABL capacity, not cash.**
  It's adequate, not comfortable, for a business that just burned cash and carries $1.1B of annual D&A and ~$700M
  of planned capex.
- **"~3x net debt/EBITDA" is management's *target*, not the current reality.** Management has stated a goal of
  **2.5x leverage with ≥$2B liquidity**, funded by (a) selling **8 non-EBITDA-contributing assets for ~$425M**
  (about $70M collected so far), 100% of proceeds plus all operating cash to debt paydown, and (b) potentially the
  **POSCO partnership** (MoU signed Q3-2025, still in due diligence, described as the #1 strategic priority for
  both sides; "substantial proceeds… to reduce indebtedness"). **On *current* earnings, leverage is not 3x — it's
  effectively double-digit/NM**, because trailing adjusted EBITDA is near zero. Net debt/EBITDA only compresses to
  ~2.5–3x if (i) the cycle recovers to ~$2.5–3B EBITDA *and* (ii) the asset sales / POSCO cash land. **Both are
  required.** This is the single biggest risk in the name: it is a levered cyclical at a cash-flow trough, and the
  deleveraging path depends on a recovery *and* a deal that may not close.
- Equity is shrinking (total Cliffs equity $6.12B → $5.82B q/q; retained deficit widened to −$766M). No dividend
  (suspended), and buybacks are off the table while delevering.

A levered equity at a cycle trough is a high-beta instrument: it works violently *up* if EBITDA recovers and debt
comes down, and it's exposed *down* if the cycle double-dips or POSCO walks. That convexity is the actual trade.

## 11. The competitive question & pricing headroom.

Can anyone else make US GOES? **Not today, and not quickly.** The metallurgy (secondary recrystallization, precise
silicon content, coating chemistry) is a multi-year, capital-heavy build; there is no announced domestic GOES
greenfield. Foreign GOES (Baowu, Nippon, POSCO, ThyssenKrupp) lands into a **50% Section-232 wall as a mill
product**, so CLF's domestic pricing headroom is, crudely, **the delivered foreign price grossed up by ~50% plus
freight and lead-time risk** — substantial. The *leak* in the wall is the finished transformer at 15% (Section 6):
a buyer can import the completed unit far more cheaply than the steel. Latent threats to watch: **Nippon Steel now
owns U.S. Steel** and could, over years, choose to bring GOES capability onshore or route Japanese GOES through
its US footprint; **Baowu just added a 320k-ton high-magnetic GOES line** (Q3-2025) expanding global supply. Near
term these don't dent the US monopoly; multi-year, they're the things that could.

## 12. Hyperscaler behavior — the demand is overwhelming; the capture is partial.

The demand side of the thesis is the strongest-verified part. **Transformer lead times have blown out to 3–5
years; >50% of planned 2026 US data centers face delay/cancellation for lack of electrical equipment; ~$650B of
2026 AI-infra capex is bottlenecked on grid gear; GSU-transformer demand grew 274% (2019→2025, Wood Mackenzie),
and the pad-mount three-phase shortage is projected to worsen through 2030.** This is a genuine, multi-year,
supply-constrained boom and CLF sits at the bottleneck. **But will hyperscalers buy domestic or import?** Both —
and that's the nuance. At a **15% finished-transformer tariff**, importing a complete unit from Korea/Mexico is
only modestly penalized, so price-sensitive buyers will still import; CLF's edge is **lead-time and supply
security** (domestic, vertically integrated, no ocean freight or geopolitical risk) more than headline price. The
shortage is so severe that *every* domestic ton CLF can make is spoken for — but the 250K-ton ceiling means CLF
captures the *premium on its slice*, not the whole $650B wave. Competitors are also adding US transformer capacity
(Hitachi Energy +$1B incl. a 2028 Virginia plant; Siemens $421M in Charlotte), which over time eases the
shortage CLF is monetizing.

## 15. Bull / base / bear — with return shapes (equity; the LEAPS is the levered overlay in Section 13).

**Bull (~2–3x equity, to ~$28–40).** The steel cycle turns convincingly through 2026–27 (ASP and volume keep
climbing, imports stay suppressed), adjusted EBITDA recovers toward **$2.5–3B+**, the **POSCO deal closes** and
with the $425M asset sales drives net debt down toward the **2.5x** target, *and* the GOES/transformer complex
gets the market to assign a separate premium multiple to the chokepoint (the "sum-of-the-parts wakes up" leg). On
a levered, de-risking balance sheet, EV/EBITDA re-rates and the **equity** does **2–3x** from ~$13.5 to the high
$20s–$40s. The LEAPS is a ~2–4x in this world. This is a real path — but note it needs **both** the cycle **and**
the deal **and** the re-rate, stacked.

**Base (~flat to +50%, ~$13–20).** The cycle grinds higher gradually (Q2 positive FCF as guided, EBITDA recovers
to ~$1–1.5B in 2026), debt comes down slowly via asset sales, POSCO drags on or closes small. The equity is worth
modestly more as the trough fear fades — call it **$15–20** — but leverage caps the multiple and GOES stays a
kicker, not a re-rate. **Note for the LEAPS:** most of this base-case band sits *at or below* the $19.75
